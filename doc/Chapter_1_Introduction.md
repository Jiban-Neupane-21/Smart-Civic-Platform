## 1.1 Introduction of the Project (In-Depth: Grounded in Nepal's Local Governance)

### 1.1.1 Background and Context of Local Governance in Nepal

#### A. Historical Evolution and the Federal Transition
Local governance in Nepal has undergone a dramatic transformation over the past three decades, shifting from a highly centralized unitary regime to a constitutionally protected federal democratic system:

1. **The Pre-2015 Unitary Era and the Local Governance Void:**
   Prior to the promulgation of the federal constitution, local administrative bodies functioned under the **Local Self-Governance Act (LSGA), 2055 (1999)** (*स्थानीय स्वायत्त शासन ऐन, २०५५*). Under this arrangement, local bodies were categorized into Village Development Committees (VDCs), Municipalities, and District Development Committees (DDCs). However, due to prolonged armed conflict and political transition, no local elections were held between 1997 and 2017—a span of nearly two decades. Local administration was overseen by unelected bureaucratic all-party mechanisms and civil servants, which created a profound democratic deficit. Public grievances regarding deteriorating civic infrastructure were frequently neglected, citizen oversight was minimal, and capital budget expenditure suffered from acute inefficiencies.

2. **The Federal Paradigm Shift (Constitution of Nepal, 2015):**
   The formal adoption of the **Constitution of Nepal in September 2015** restructured the state into three autonomous yet interdependent tiers of government: the **Federal (*संघ*)**, **Provincial (*प्रदेश*)**, and **Local (*स्थानीय*)** levels. Unlike conventional decentralization models where local bodies merely execute policies delegated by central ministries, the Constitution of Nepal institutionalizes local levels as self-governing governments possessing distinct constitutional standing, independent legislative assemblies, executive bodies, and quasi-judicial authority.

3. **Restructuring into 753 Autonomous Local Levels:**
   Pursuant to the recommendations of the Local Level Restructuring Commission (LLRC), Nepal restructured its former patchwork of over 3,900 VDCs and small municipalities into **753 consolidated Local Levels (*स्थानीय तह*)**:
   * **6 Metropolitan Cities (*महानगरपालिका*)** — e.g., Kathmandu, Lalitpur, Pokhara, Bharatpur, Biratnagar, and Birgunj, representing dense urban, economic, and commercial hubs.
   * **11 Sub-Metropolitan Cities (*उपमहानगरपालिका*)** — regional growth centers such as Dharan, Itahari, Janakpur, Hetauda, Butwal, Tulsipur, Ghorahi, Nepalgunj, Dhangadhi, Kalaiya, and Jitpur Simara.
   * **276 Municipalities (*नगरपालिका*)** — urban and semi-urban municipal councils.
   * **460 Rural Municipalities (*गाउँपालिका*)** — administrative units serving rural and peri-urban populations.

#### B. Constitutional Mandate and the Local Government Operation Act, 2074
The statutory and legal foundation governing civic service delivery in Nepal is codified under two central legal instruments:

1. **Constitutional Powers under Schedule 8 and Article 57(4):**
   Schedule 8 of the Constitution outlines the **22 Exclusive Powers of the Local Level (*स्थानीय तहको एकल अधिकारको सूची*)**. This provision explicitly tasks local governments with municipal police regulation, local tax collection, public utility management, municipal road construction, drinking water supply, sewage and stormwater drainage, solid waste management, environmental conservation, and local disaster response.

2. **The Local Government Operation Act, 2074 (2017) (*स्थानीय सरकार सञ्चालन ऐन, २०७४*):**
   Enacted by the Federal Parliament to operationalize the constitutional provisions, this legislation delineates the internal administrative organization, operational procedures, and statutory duties of municipal executives. Key sections mandate:
   * **Section 11:** Defines the comprehensive functions, duties, and rights of municipalities across infrastructure, public health, disaster preparedness, and social welfare.
   * **Section 12:** Delineates the frontline responsibilities of **Ward Committees (*वडा समिति*)**, obligating them to maintain civic infrastructure, monitor public cleanliness, inspect developmental activities, and maintain direct contact with local communities.
   * **Section 14:** Establishes grievance registration mechanisms, citizens' charters (*नागरिक बडापत्र*), and public audit requirements to guarantee administrative accountability.

#### C. Grassroots Decentralization: The Ward Office as the Frontline Interface
In the administrative architecture of Nepal, the **Ward Office (*वडा कार्यालय*)** is the most vital, grassroots unit of public administration. Each municipality is subdivided into geographically demarcated wards (e.g., Kathmandu Metropolitan City has 32 wards; Pokhara Metropolitan City has 33 wards):

* **Ward Committee Composition:** Each ward is governed by an elected Ward Committee comprising the **Ward Chairperson (*वडा अध्यक्ष*)** and four elected ward members (including two mandatory women members, one representing the Dalit community). The executive staff is led by the **Ward Secretary (*वडा सचिव*)**, who coordinates with municipal engineers and technical overseers.
* **Frontline Role in Civic Life:** The ward office is the primary institutional point where citizens obtain vital event registrations (birth, death, marriage), residential certificates, property recommendations (*सिफारिस*), and report municipal breakdowns. 

Despite this decentralized proximity, most ward offices remain technologically disconnected from central municipal departments. When a citizen reports a damaged road or blocked sewer at the ward office, staff rely on manual written registers, personal telephone calls, or paper memos forwarded to the municipal executive building, introducing significant bureaucratic friction and tracking delays.

#### D. Core Civic Utility Sectors and Real-World Municipal Realities in Nepal
The delivery of municipal services in Nepal is organized around five critical civil engineering and utility domains:

1. **Urban Road Networks and Infrastructure Maintenance (*सडक तथा पूर्वाधार विकास शाखा*):**
   Municipal roads across Nepal suffer rapid deterioration due to high traffic volumes, substandard construction materials, monsoon erosion, and frequent uncoordinated trenching by utility agencies. Potholes (*खाल्डाखुल्डी*), missing manhole covers, cracked footpaths, and collapsed retaining walls represent major traffic hazards and safety liabilities that require immediate municipal intervention.

2. **Solid Waste Management & Sanitation (*फोहोरमैला व्यवस्थापन तथा वातावरण शाखा*):**
   Governed under the **Solid Waste Management Act, 2068 (2011)**, local bodies are solely responsible for urban sanitation. In metropolitan areas like Kathmandu, solid waste management is an acute, recurring public crisis. Irregular door-to-door waste collection leads to unauthorized curbside dumping along riverbanks (e.g., Bagmati and Bishnumati corridors) and road corners. The lack of an automated civic reporting channel prevents municipal sanitation supervisors from promptly detecting and clearing spontaneous open dumps.

3. **Drinking Water Supply & Pipeline Maintenance (*खानेपानी तथा सरसफाइ शाखा*):**
   In urban centers, drinking water distribution involves a complex web of agencies, including municipal water branches, public utility boards like **Kathmandu Upatyaka Khanepani Limited (KUKL)**, the Melamchi Water Supply Project, and community-managed Water Users and Sanitation Committees (WUSCs / *खानेपानी उपभोक्ता समिति*). Unmonitored pipe bursts lead to severe road subsidence, massive clean water wastage, and contaminated domestic water supplies.

4. **Stormwater Drainage, Sewerage & Monsoon Flood Mitigation (*ढल निकास तथा डुबान नियन्त्रण*):**
   Nepal experiences intense precipitation during the South Asian summer monsoon (June to September). Insufficiently dimensioned or clogged urban drains cause severe flash floods and waterlogging across urban streets (e.g., Putalisadak, Maitighar, and river corridor roads in Kathmandu; Mahendrapool in Pokhara). Rapid reporting and clearance of blocked drainage culverts before heavy downpours is an essential municipal duty.

5. **Street Lighting, Electrical Safety & Cable Management (*सडक बत्ती तथा सहरी सौन्दर्यीकरण*):**
   Municipalities have increasingly installed high-mast, LED, and solar streetlights along primary arterial roads. However, non-functional streetlights frequently remain unrepaired for months due to a lack of maintenance logging. Furthermore, hazardous webs of tangled, low-hanging overhead electrical, cable television, and optical fiber wires (*लथालिङ्ग तारहरू*) clutter utility poles across Nepali cities, creating acute fire and pedestrian electrocution risks.

#### E. Alignment with the Digital Nepal Framework and E-Governance Mandates
Recognizing the limitations of paper-based governance, the Government of Nepal introduced the **Digital Nepal Framework (2019)** (*डिजिटल नेपाल फ्रेमवर्क*), spearheaded by the Ministry of Communication and Information Technology (MoCIT). The framework outlines an ambitious vision to leverage Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) across eight primary sectors, prominently including:
* **Digital Governance:** Delivering transparent, accessible, paperless, and citizen-friendly public services online.
* **Smart Cities & Urban Infrastructure:** Deploying GIS, automated telemetry, and digital grievance management to build resilient, data-driven municipalities.

The **Smart Civic Platform** directly operationalizes the core objectives of the Digital Nepal Framework. By replacing antiquated manual petitions with an integrated web platform featuring geospatial boundary detection, role-based workflows, and verifiable proof of resolution, the project offers a timely, scalable, and constitutionally aligned solution for local governance in federal Nepal.

### 1.1.2 Contemporary Challenges in Nepal's Urban Municipalities
Despite federal decentralization, rapid and largely unplanned urbanization—most acutely visible across the Kathmandu Valley (Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur), Pokhara, Bharatpur, Biratnagar, Birgunj, and the expanding industrial corridors of the Terai—has overwhelmed the operational capacities of municipal authorities. 

The day-to-day reality of civic service delivery in Nepal reveals critical systemic deficiencies:
1. **Burdensome Paper-Based Grievance Mechanisms:**
   Traditionally, lodging a civic complaint requires a citizen to take time off work, travel in person to their local Ward Office (*वडा कार्यालय*) or Municipal Headquarters (*नगरपालिका*), purchase revenue stamps, draft a formal paper petition (*हस्तलिखित निवेदन*), secure official registration (*दर्ता*), and obtain official administrative endorsement (*तोक लगाउने*).
2. **Fragmentation Across Municipal Tiers:**
   Petitions frequently bounce back and forth between the Ward Secretary (*वडा सचिव*), Ward Chairperson (*वडा अध्यक्ष*), Chief Administrative Officer (*प्रमुख प्रशासकीय अधिकृत - CAO*), and relevant Departmental Engineers/Overseers (*इन्जिनियर तथा प्राविधिक ओभरसियर*), with no centralized tracking mechanism.
3. **Limitations of Centralized Portals (e.g., *Hello Sarkar*):**
   While central grievance redressal initiatives such as *Hello Sarkar* (*हेलो सरकार*) exist under the Office of the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers (OPMCM), they operate at a macro federal level. They lack real-time municipal ward resolution, fine-grained geospatial mapping, internal field-crew dispatch systems, and localized operational dashboards for municipal engineers and ward supervisors.
4. **Queue Congestion from Redundant Public Grievances:**
   When a civic breakdown occurs—such as a collapsed drainage cover on a bustling street in New Baneshwor, Kathmandu, or an overflowing garbage dump in Chipledhunga, Pokhara—dozens of conscious citizens independently attempt to report the incident. Lacking spatiotemporal deduplication, municipal staff are flooded with duplicate reports, distorting workload metrics and misallocating scarce technical personnel.
5. **Absence of Verifiable Work Completion:**
   Civic grievances in Nepal are frequently closed administratively without empirical verification. Citizens are left dissatisfied when a complaint is marked "resolved" on paper while the physical pothole or water leak remains untouched on site.

### 1.1.3 Overview and Concept of the Smart Civic Platform

Developed using a modern web architecture (React, TypeScript, Node.js/Express, and PostgreSQL/Supabase), the system transforms civic grievance handling into an automated, transparent, closed-loop digital ecosystem.

* **1. Superadmin (National/System Administrator):**
  Manages system-level provisioning, registers new municipalities into the platform (e.g., Kathmandu Metropolitan City, Lalitpur Metropolitan City, Pokhara Metropolitan City, etc.), and monitors global database health, API rate limits, and audit logs.
* **2. Municipality Head (*नगर प्रमुख / उपप्रमुख / प्रमुख प्रशासकीय अधिकृत - CAO*):**
  Represents executive leadership. Monitors cross-departmental operations, reviews city-wide SLA compliance rates, investigates administrative escalations, and oversees the verification of citizen identities through official documentation (e.g., Nepali Citizenship Certificate / *नागरिकता प्रमाणपत्र* or National Identity Card / *राष्ट्रिय परिचयपत्र*).
* **3. Department Head (*शाखा प्रमुख / वरिष्ठ इन्जिनियर*):**
  Oversees specialized municipal branches (Infrastructure, Sanitation, Water Supply, Electricity). Reviews incoming grievance queues, utilizes automated workload metrics to assign tasks to field staff, re-routes misclassified issues, and monitors SLA timers.
* **4. Field Staff (*प्राविधिक कर्मचारी / ओभरसियर / फिल्ड टोली*):**
  Operational crews who receive assigned work orders on their mobile/desktop dashboards. Upon visiting the grievance site and completing civil repairs, they must upload geocoded completion notes and mandatory post-repair photographs to substantiate resolution.
* **5. Citizen (*नगरबासी / सेवाग्राही*):**
  Residents who lodge grievances within seconds using interactive maps, monitor real-time ticket progress from submission to resolution, upvote existing neighborhood issues to avoid duplicate filings, and rate municipal service quality upon ticket closure.

### 1.1.5 Core Principles & Technological Pillars
The Smart Civic Platform is anchored upon five foundational engineering principles designed to meet the rigors of modern civic administration in Nepal:

1. **Geospatial Intelligence & Ward Boundary Resolution:**
   Every civic complaint is anchored to verified latitude and longitude coordinates. Utilizing Point-in-Polygon (Ray-Casting) algorithms and reverse-geocoding, the system automatically resolves the exact municipal ward boundary, eliminating the human error of filing complaints in incorrect wards.
2. **Spatiotemporal Deduplication & Community Upvoting:**
   To combat queue congestion, the platform computes geodesic distances (Haversine formula) and text similarity across open complaints. If a neighbor has already reported a pothole or water leak within a 300-meter radius in the last 72 hours, the citizen is prompted to **"+1 Upvote / Me Too"** the existing ticket instead of creating a redundant entry.
3. **Predictive SLA Management & Accountability:**
   Grievances operate under automated Service Level Agreements based on severity (Urgent: 24h, High: 48h, Medium: 72h, Low: 120h). The platform features an early warning engine that alerts department heads *before* deadlines lapse, preventing administrative neglect.
4. **Fraud-Resistant Proof of Resolution:**
   Field personnel cannot close a ticket with arbitrary status clicks. Mandatory submission of on-site resolution photographs and completion notes ensures genuine field execution and restores citizen confidence in municipal administration.
5. **Secure, Scalable & Resilient Architecture:**
   Engineered with stateless JSON Web Tokens (JWT), PostgreSQL Row-Level Security (RLS), and responsive client interfaces optimized for standard 4G/mobile networks prevalent across urban and semi-urban Nepal.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Despite continuous efforts to modernize public service delivery, municipal grievance redressal systems suffer from systemic operational and technical bottlenecks:

1. **Cumbersome and Fragmented Reporting:**
   Citizens lack a unified, accessible digital portal to submit complaints with rich contextual data (high-resolution photographic evidence, automated GPS coordinates, and detailed descriptions). Traditional phone helplines or physical suggestion boxes do not offer verifiable audit trails.

2. **Duplicate Ticket Overload & Queue Congestion:**
   When visible civic infrastructure fails (e.g., a collapsed drainage cover on a major street junction), multiple residents independently report the exact same issue. Without intelligent spatiotemporal deduplication, municipal departmental queues become overwhelmed with redundant tickets, fragmenting communication and misrepresenting actual grievance volume.

3. **Inaccurate Category & Departmental Routing:**
   Citizens often lack insight into internal municipal hierarchies and departmental demarcations. Grievances are frequently filed under erroneous departments, causing protracted cross-departmental ping-pong delays before reaching the appropriate operational unit.

4. **Lack of Transparency and Asymmetric Information:**
   Once a grievance is submitted, citizens are typically left without visibility into ticket progress. The absence of status milestones (Received $\rightarrow$ Assigned $\rightarrow$ In Progress $\rightarrow$ Resolved) breeds public skepticism and diminishes civic engagement.

5. **Inefficient Field Dispatch and Absence of SLA Tracking:**
   Department heads manually assign tasks without real-time visibility into staff workload, geographic proximity, or specialization. Furthermore, grievance resolution deadlines are unmonitored or handled reactively only after citizens escalate complaints.

6. **Absence of Empirical Civic Analytics:**
   Municipal leadership lacks aggregated spatiotemporal data to identify systemic infrastructure hotspots, monitor departmental resolution velocity, or make data-driven capital allocation decisions.

## 1.3 Objectives of the Project

### 1.3.1 General Objective
The primary objective of this project is to design, develop, test, and deploy a comprehensive, full-stack **Smart Civic Platform** that digitalizes and automates civic grievance redressal, enhances administrative transparency, and optimizes municipal field operations through intelligent routing and real-time tracking.

### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives
To achieve the general objective, the project pursues the following specific technical and operational goals:

1. **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC):**
   Implement secure, fine-grained access control supporting five distinct stakeholder roles: **Citizen**, **Field Staff**, **Department Head**, **Municipality Head**, and **Superadmin**.
2. **Automated Location Resolution:**
   Integrate geospatial reverse-geocoding and Point-in-Polygon (Ray-Casting) algorithms to automatically resolve a citizen's GPS coordinates to the respective municipality and administrative ward.
3. **Automated Complaint Lifecycle Management:**
   Develop an automated ticketing lifecycle that generates unique tracking identifiers, enforces automated SLA deadlines (Urgent: 24h, High: 48h, Medium: 72h, Low: 120h), and maps complaints to responsible municipal departments.
4. **Deduplication and Upvoting:**
   Incorporate spatiotemporal proximity algorithms to identify near-duplicate complaints within a geographical radius and allow citizens to upvote existing issues rather than flooding the system with duplicate tickets.
5. **Operational Dashboards & Analytics:**
   Design responsive, role-specific operational dashboards that present real-time ticket queues, SLA compliance rates, staff workload distributions, and spatial grievance density heatmaps.
6. **Real-Time Notification Engine:**
   Engineer a multi-channel notification architecture (in-app polling and alerts) that dispatches instant updates to citizens and municipal staff upon status transitions, assignments, and escalations.
7. **Resolution Verification & Accountability:**
   Provide verification mechanisms requiring field staff to upload photo evidence and geocoded completion notes before a grievance ticket can transition to the resolved state.

## 1.4 Scope and Limitations of the Project

### 1.4.1 Scope of the Project
The system encompasses the following functional and technological boundaries:

* **Geographical & Organizational Scope:**
  Supports multi-tenant municipal configurations where a Superadmin can provision multiple municipalities, each configured with unique wards, municipal departments (e.g., Sanitation, Public Works, Water Supply, Electricity), and associated staff hierarchies.
* **Functional Scope:**
  * Citizen onboarding, identity/KYC profile completion, and credential management.
  * Grievance submission with title, description, category, photographic attachment, and interactive GPS map selection.
  * Public complaint tracking via unique alphanumeric tracking IDs.
  * Departmental queue management, automated workload balancing, and manual re-routing.
  * Field staff assignment, status updating, and proof-of-work submission.
  * Analytical reporting, SLA breach forecasting, and exportable grievance summaries.
* **Technical Stack:**
  * **Frontend:** React 18, Vite, TypeScript, Material UI / Tailwind CSS, Axios, Lucide/MUI Icons.
  * **Backend:** Node.js, Express.js, TypeScript, RESTful API architecture, JSON Web Tokens (JWT), Helmet, CORS, and Express Validator.
  * **Database & Storage:** Supabase (PostgreSQL 15+), Supabase Auth, Row-Level Security (RLS), and Supabase Storage Buckets for document and media hosting.

### 1.4.2 Limitations of the Project
While the system provides an end-to-end operational framework, certain boundaries and assumptions apply:

1. **Network Connectivity Dependency:**
   The platform requires an active internet connection. Offline grievance caching and deferred synchronization are outside the initial project release scope.
2. **GPS Accuracy Variance:**
   Geospatial resolution relies on client device GPS accuracy and browser location permissions. In dense urban canyons or low-end hardware, location precision may exhibit slight drift.
3. **Third-Party Telephony (SMS Gateway):**
   SMS OTP verification is architected and tested using gateway interfaces; production SMS dispatch is subject to third-party telecommunication API credits and gateway uptime.
4. **Language Localization:**
   The primary application interface is developed in English. Multi-lingual regional localization (e.g., Nepali / Devanagari script) is designed as a modular future enhancement.

## 1.6 Development Methodology (In-Depth Project Engineering)

To engineer the **Smart Civic Platform**, the **Agile Software Development Methodology**—specifically the **Scrum Framework coupled with Iterative Prototyping**—was adopted. 

In public-sector municipal software engineering, requirements are rarely static. A civic platform involves heterogeneous user groups (citizens, ward secretaries, municipal department engineers, field crews, and executive leadership) who possess varying degrees of technical literacy and disparate operational objectives. The Agile Scrum model provides tangible architectural advantages for this project:
1. **Iterative Prototyping & Feedback Loops:** Enabled rapid development of functional modules (e.g., citizen complaint submission forms) that could be evaluated and refined before implementing complex backend automation.
2. **Decomposition of Multi-Role Workflows:** Allowed the isolated engineering and testing of five discrete role-based sub-systems (Citizen, Field Staff, Department Head, Municipality Head, and Superadmin).
3. **Risk Mitigation in Algorithmic Implementation:** Provided dedicated sprint buffers to independently test and tune complex mathematical routines—such as the Haversine spatiotemporal deduplication filter and the Ray-Casting ward resolver—prior to end-to-end integration.

### 1.6.2 Detailed Breakdown of System Development Lifecycle (SDLC) Phases

The project’s development lifecycle was executed across five structured, chronological phases:

#### Phase 1: Problem Domain Research and Requirements Engineering
* **Field Investigation & Stakeholder Mapping:** Investigated typical municipal workflows across urban centers in Nepal (e.g., Kathmandu and Pokhara), identifying the operational barriers of paper petitions, lack of photographic verification, and queue congestion caused by duplicate complaints.
* **Requirements Specification:** Categorized system needs into Functional Requirements (e.g., JWT authentication, reverse-geocoding, automated SLA tracking, file uploads) and Non-Functional Requirements (e.g., sub-second API response times, sub-30s notification polling, mobile responsiveness, and data security).
* **Product Backlog Formulation:** Documented user stories formatted from stakeholder perspectives (e.g., *"As a Department Head, I want to view the current active workload of all field technicians, so that I can dispatch urgent tasks without overloading any single staff member"*).

#### Phase 2: Architectural and System Design
* **Tiered Architectural Pattern:** Established a decoupled client-server architecture:
  * **Client Layer:** Single Page Application (SPA) developed with React 18, TypeScript, and Vite, leveraging modular component hierarchies and client-side route guards.
  * **Application Server Layer:** RESTful API service developed using Node.js and Express.js in TypeScript, enforcing layered separation of concerns: Routes $\rightarrow$ Controllers $\rightarrow$ Services $\rightarrow$ Repositories.
  * **Data and Storage Layer:** Supabase-managed PostgreSQL relational database, utilizing Row-Level Security (RLS) policies and dedicated S3-compatible cloud storage buckets (`complaint-media`, `identity-docs`).
* **Database Relational Modeling (ERD):** Engineered normalized relational schemas in `Supabase_Schema.sql`, establishing entity relationships across `profiles`, `citizens`, `municipalities`, `departments`, `complaints`, `assignments`, `sla_logs`, `upvotes`, and `notifications`.
* **Interface & Interaction Modeling:** Designed UI wireframes and user interaction flows using Material UI (MUI) design patterns, ensuring clear typographic hierarchy, intuitive status badges, and accessible touch targets on mobile viewports.

#### Phase 3: Sprint-Based Iterative Implementation
Development was executed across five sequential, milestone-driven Sprints:

* **Sprint 1: Multi-Tenant RBAC Foundation, Authentication & Onboarding**
  * Configured Express server middleware: CORS, Helmet security headers, rate limiting, and global error handlers.

## 1.7 Operational Definition of Terms and Acronyms

* **API (Application Programming Interface):** A set of protocols and communication routines enabling frontend and backend services to exchange data.
* **CORS (Cross-Origin Resource Sharing):** A browser-enforced security mechanism controlling resource access across different origins.
* **CRUD (Create, Read, Update, Delete):** The four fundamental functions of persistent database storage.
* **GIS (Geographic Information System):** A framework for capturing, managing, analyzing, and visualizing spatial and geographic data.
* **Haversine Formula:** A mathematical equation calculating great-circle distances between two pairs of latitude/longitude coordinates on a sphere.
* **JWT (JSON Web Token):** A compact, URL-safe standard used for securely transmitting claims between parties for user authentication.
* **KYC (Know Your Customer / Citizen Verification):** An administrative validation process confirming user identity via official documents (e.g., Citizenship Card, National ID).
* **RBAC (Role-Based Access Control):** An access control paradigm restricting system functionality based on assigned organizational roles.
* **RLS (Row-Level Security):** A PostgreSQL security capability restricting which database rows a user query can access or mutate.
* **SLA (Service Level Agreement):** A defined operational commitment establishing maximum permissible time thresholds for grievance resolution.
